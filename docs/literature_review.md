# Literature Review and Scientific Grounding

## A. Early-onset colorectal cancer: background and definitions

Colorectal cancer (CRC) incidence in patients over 50 has been declining for decades, largely attributed to screening uptake, while incidence in adults under 50 — early-onset CRC (EOCRC) — has been rising steadily since the late 1980s in high-income countries (Akimoto et al., 2021, *Nature Reviews Clinical Oncology*). In the US, EOCRC accounted for approximately 13.8% of new CRC diagnoses in 2022, prompting the American Cancer Society to lower its recommended screening age to 45.

**Age-cutoff inconsistency is a documented, acknowledged limitation across this literature, not an issue specific to this project.** The REACCT Collaborative's review (Zaborowski et al., 2021, *JAMA Surgery*) explicitly states that <50 years is the conventional EOCRC threshold primarily because it aligns with historical screening-program start ages, and separately notes that dichotomizing a continuous variable (age) at an arbitrary integer cutoff is itself a real methodological limitation of the field, not just of any single study. This project's own choice of thresholds is dictated by how each source dataset happened to define its groups. **GSE213092** compares EOCRC (<50) against LOCRC defined narrowly as >70 — a wide, non-adjacent age gap. **TCGA-COAD was evaluated using <50 years as EOCRC and ≥50 years as LOCRC** — a dichotomization of a continuous age variable that, unlike GSE213092, places patients in their early 50s in the same LOCRC category as patients in their late 80s. This is a real, disclosed cross-cohort comparability limitation, not merely a labeling inconsistency: TCGA's LOCRC group spans the full older-age range with many patients close to the 50-year boundary, whereas GSE213092's LOCRC patients are all at least 71 years old. Any difference in how "clean" a signal appears between the two cohorts should be read partly in light of this difference in age-group construction, not attributed to cohort quality alone.

EOCRC is reported to present at more advanced stages with more aggressive histopathological features, and while genetic predisposition syndromes (Lynch syndrome, familial adenomatous polyposis) account for a recognized minority of cases, most EOCRC is sporadic, with proposed but unconfirmed environmental contributors (diet, obesity, antibiotic use, gut microbiome shifts) under active investigation (Akimoto et al., 2021; PMC review, 2025). Age-associated molecular differences between EOCRC and LOCRC have been reported across multiple molecular modalities, although the consistency and biological interpretation of these differences remain under investigation — this project's research question concerns the discriminability and cross-cohort robustness of a transcriptomic signal specifically, not a claim that EOCRC and LOCRC are categorically distinct disease entities.

## B. Transcriptomic studies of EOCRC vs. LOCRC

The most directly relevant primary studies to this project:

- **Marx et al. (2024, *Frontiers in Oncology*)** — RNA-seq of patient-matched tumor/adjacent-normal tissue, EOCRC (n=21, GSE196006) vs. LOCRC (n=22, GSE251845), matched on demographic/clinical variables. Used TCGA-COAD for validation of specific findings. Identified an 8-gene set (ALDOB, FBXL16, IL1RN, MSLN, RAC3, SLC38A11, WBSCR27, WNT11) and reported cell-type-deconvolution-based immune-population differences and splicing-machinery differences between groups. This is the source of the externally nominated panel evaluated in this project (see Theme C).
- **Yochum lab / earlier MYC study (2022, *Biomolecules*)** — the predecessor study using GSE196006, identifying MYC/PVT1 dysregulation as an EOCRC-associated axis, illustrating that EOCRC transcriptomic findings have varied across analyses even within overlapping cohorts and research groups.
- Multiple more recent reanalysis papers (e.g., Al-Madhagi, 2023, *Informatics in Medicine Unlocked*; Nguyen et al., 2025, *Frontiers in Oncology*) apply GEO2R, cBioPortal, or GEPIA-based differential expression and hub-gene network analysis to existing public data. These are valuable for hypothesis generation but are secondary reanalyses of already-deposited data, not new cohorts. **Note on our own search claim:** our targeted search (2024–2026 window) did not identify a publicly accessible, independent, genome-wide bulk RNA-seq cohort with directly defined EOCRC/LOCRC labels, adequate sample size, and sufficient cross-cohort compatibility for inclusion in this study. This is reported as the outcome of a specific, bounded search — not as a claim that no such dataset exists anywhere, which a search of this scope cannot establish.

**A consistent distinction across this literature, and one this project deliberately maintains:** identifying differentially expressed genes between groups, building a prognostic/survival signature, and building an age-group classifier are three different analytical tasks with different endpoints, even when applied to the same underlying data. Marx et al.'s 8 genes were derived in service of the first two tasks (tumor-vs-normal contrast comparison and a survival-predictive score), not the third — a distinction this project's terminology (Section C, and throughout the results) deliberately preserves rather than collapsing into "the EOCRC signature."

## C. The Marx et al. 8-gene panel: what it actually is

Based on direct inspection of the Marx et al. (2024) abstract and available text: the study's endpoint for the 8 genes was **a score predictive of overall CRC patient survival**, developed from genes initially identified through an EOCRC-vs-LOCRC tumor/normal contrast comparison — not a classifier trained or validated for the EOCRC-vs-LOCRC task itself. The paper does not report the exact fitted coefficients or gene-direction weights of that survival score in a form independently reconstructable from available text and search.

**This project's explicit handling, consistent with this literature finding:** the 8 gene *identities* are treated as an externally nominated candidate set; a new classifier is fit using only these genes for the EOCRC-vs-LOCRC task, using this project's own evaluation framework. The result (Table 2: discovery AUC 0.591, external AUC 0.540, not statistically significant) should be read as evidence about **whether this specific gene identity set transfers to a different endpoint, representation, and cohort than its original context** — not as a test of, replication of, or refutation of Marx et al.'s original survival-prediction finding, which this project does not attempt to reproduce.

## D. Machine learning on high-dimensional bulk transcriptomic data

Gene expression data is characteristically high-dimensional relative to sample size (p >> n) — this project's discovery cohort has 33,115 candidate genes and 98 patients. This p >> n structure is what necessitates regularization in the first place, independent of any specific literature recommendation: without it, a linear model has enough free parameters to fit training-set noise rather than signal. **Elastic Net** (Zou & Hastie, 2005, *Journal of the Royal Statistical Society Series B*) is used here as a defensible, interpretable baseline that combines L1 (sparsity-inducing) and L2 (ridge/stability-inducing) penalties — a reasoned methodological choice given the data's structure, rather than a method adopted simply because prior literature also happens to use it.

A separate, well-documented risk in this literature is **selection bias from improper cross-validation**: performing feature selection or hyperparameter tuning outside of, or only partially within, cross-validation folds produces optimistically biased performance estimates (Cawley & Talbot, 2010, *Journal of Machine Learning Research*, "On Over-fitting in Model Selection and Subsequent Selection Bias in Performance Evaluation"). This project's insistence on patient-level, nested cross-validation — feature selection, scaling, and hyperparameter tuning computed strictly within training folds at every stage — is a direct methodological response to this well-established risk, not an arbitrary rigor preference.

This literature also supports the project's core interpretive stance: a modest internal AUC that survives independent external validation is more scientifically informative than a very high internal AUC obtained without external validation, precisely because the latter is exactly the situation in which confounding or overfitting cannot be ruled out (see Theme E).

## E. Batch effects, confounding, and the necessity of cross-cohort validation

This is the literature most directly relevant to this project's central methodological event — the rejection of the GSE251845+GSE196006 pair.

**Leek et al. (2010, *Nature Reviews Genetics*)**, "Tackling the widespread and critical impact of batch effects in high-throughput data," is the foundational reference establishing that technical batch effects (reagent lots, personnel, processing dates, and by extension platform/protocol differences) are pervasive in high-throughput genomic data and become a serious problem specifically when correlated with the outcome of interest — precisely the situation this project found between age group and sequencing platform in the rejected dataset pair.

**Soneson, Gerster & Delorenzi (2014, *PLOS ONE*)**, "Batch Effect Confounding Leads to Strong Bias in Performance Estimates Obtained by Cross-Validation," directly demonstrates via simulation that when batch and outcome are confounded, standard cross-validation performance estimates become severely biased upward — a close, direct methodological parallel to what this project observed empirically (the ~100% classification result) and subsequently diagnosed and rejected.

Separately, multiple studies (cited within Robustifying genomic classifiers to batch effects via ensemble learning, bioRxiv) report that **cross-study/cross-cohort validation performance of genomic classifiers is consistently found to be inferior to internal cross-validation performance**, and that this gap is not fully explained by the most obvious sources of heterogeneity — directly supporting this project's design decision to treat external validation (TCGA-COAD) as more informative evidence than the discovery-cohort cross-validation estimate, and to expect (not be alarmed by) some drop in performance between the two.

**Internal validation and external validation are not the same test, and this distinction is the direct conceptual basis for this project's design.** In the clinical prediction modeling literature, internal validation (cross-validation, bootstrapping) assesses reproducibility and overfitting within the population a model was developed on; external validation on independently collected data assesses **transportability** — whether a learned relationship remains useful when applied to data generated under different, related-but-not-identical conditions (Van Calster, Steyerberg, Wynants & van Smeden, 2023, *BMC Medicine*, "There is no such thing as a validated prediction model"). This same paper argues that favorable performance at one external validation should not be read as proof of universal applicability, since patient populations, measurement procedures, and case mix genuinely vary across settings — a caution this project's own framing of its TCGA-COAD result (a modest, statistically detectable, but not "clinically validated" signal) is written to respect.

This project's use of a **permutation test specifically on external-validation discrimination** (10,000 label shuffles against the frozen model's fixed TCGA predictions) has direct precedent: Nieboer, van der Ploeg & Steyerberg (2016, *PLOS ONE*) evaluate permutation testing of the discrimination statistic (c-statistic/AUC) at external validation as a method for judging model transportability. **Their own finding is an important caveat this project should not omit**: such permutation tests can be misleading when there are substantial case-mix differences between the development and validation populations, since the test may reflect population heterogeneity rather than model correctness. This is directly relevant here — TCGA-COAD and GSE213092 differ in age-group construction (see Theme A), class balance, and site heterogeneity — meaning our external permutation result (p=0.001 for Representation A) should be read as evidence that the frozen predictions retain real association with the true labels in a genuinely different population, which is itself a meaningful and non-trivial finding, but not as a claim that case-mix differences between the two cohorts have been fully accounted for. This general concept — that a model's behavior under a new data distribution is a distinct question from its fit to its original data — is formalized more broadly in the machine learning literature on dataset shift (Quiñonero-Candela et al., 2008, *Dataset Shift in Machine Learning*, MIT Press).

## F. Bulk RNA-seq and cellular composition

Bulk RNA-seq measures a summed transcriptional signal across all cell types present in a tissue sample and, unlike single-cell RNA-seq, does not directly retain information about which cells contributed which transcripts (Chu et al., 2023, *Genome Biology*; Danko et al., *Nature Cancer*, BayesPrism). Computational deconvolution methods (CIBERSORTx, MuSiC, BayesPrism, BLADE) exist to estimate cell-type proportions from bulk data using reference signatures, but these methods carry their own substantial methodological assumptions and limitations — closely related cell types can share marker genes, and reference-vs-bulk platform/distributional mismatches can bias estimates (Lu et al., 2021, *Nature Communications*, BLADE).

**This literature directly supports two decisions in this project**: first, the decision not to attempt full computational deconvolution, given its own non-trivial methodological assumptions and the scope constraints of this study; second, and more importantly, the insistence throughout the biological interpretation (Sections 26–36 of the frozen record) that marker-gene associations be described as exactly that — genes "associated with" a cellular compartment — rather than as measured cell-type proportions or confirmed tumor-cell-intrinsic biology. The absence of canonical epithelial markers (EPCAM, KRT8, KRT18) and presence of stromal/vascular/muscle markers in this project's stable gene set is a real, quantified finding, but the literature is clear that resolving it into an actual composition claim would require the reference-based methods this project did not attempt.

## G. Pathway-level representations

The MSigDB Hallmark gene set collection (Liberzon et al., 2015, *Cell Systems*) was built by identifying gene sets with coherent, non-redundant biological themes from broader, more overlapping collections (e.g., GO, KEGG) — the design goal being fewer, more interpretable, less mutually-redundant gene sets, which motivated this project's choice of Hallmark over a larger, more granular pathway database (Section 19 of the frozen record) given the discovery cohort's small sample size.

This project deliberately implemented pathway scoring as a simple mean gene-set z-score rather than ssGSEA (Barbie et al., 2009) or GSVA (Hänzelmann et al., 2013) — chosen for transparency and independent verifiability in this environment rather than because it is claimed to be the superior scoring method. The literature on pathway-informed machine learning broadly supports the *rationale* for testing a pathway-level representation (biological structure may provide a more robust, lower-dimensional signal than raw genes), but this project's negative result for Representation B should be read as evidence about this specific scoring method on this specific task, not as a general statement about pathway-based methods.

## H. Pretrained/foundation models for bulk RNA-seq (brief — see note on placement below)

Foundation models pretrained on large transcriptomic corpora are a recent, active area of methods development. **BulkFormer** (Kang et al., 2026, *Cell Systems*) and **BulkRNABert** (Gélard et al., 2025, *PMLR 259*) are both bulk-RNA-specific pretrained transformers, motivated by the observation that most prior transcriptomic foundation models (Geneformer, scGPT, scFoundation) were pretrained on single-cell data with different gene-coverage and sparsity characteristics than bulk tissue profiles — making bulk-specific pretraining the theoretically appropriate choice for this project's data modality, in principle.

**This project attempted both, and neither produced a usable quantitative result**, due to independently-verified, currently-active third-party software infrastructure incompatibilities (detailed in the frozen project record and `docs/decision_log.md`) rather than any issue with the models themselves.

**Placement note:** because this investigation did not produce a quantitative representation to compare against A, B, and the Marx panel, it should not receive equal prominence to those three in the final report's Introduction. The fuller technical account belongs in Methods (as an attempted, documented, non-quantitative extension) and/or Discussion (as a limitation and direction for future work), not framed as a fourth core representation in the main narrative.

## Research Gap Statement

Existing studies have identified molecular differences associated with age at colorectal cancer diagnosis, including specific gene sets, immune-population differences, and splicing alterations (Marx et al., 2024, and related work). However, these findings have generally been established within a single cohort or a small set of matched cohorts, using a single representation of the transcriptome (typically direct differential expression), and — as this project's own dataset audit demonstrates — without always ruling out technical confounding between the biological label of interest and cohort/platform variables. It remains less clear how robust an age-associated transcriptomic signal is when the same underlying data is represented at different biological levels (individual genes, curated pathway scores, a compact externally-published gene panel) and evaluated not just within a discovery cohort but against an independent external cohort. This project's contribution is a controlled empirical comparison showing how representation choice and cross-cohort validation affect the apparent robustness of an EOCRC-vs-LOCRC transcriptomic signal — motivated directly by a real methodological failure (the rejected confounded dataset) that itself illustrates why such controls matter. This is offered as a specific, bounded contribution, not a claim of unprecedented novelty; where directly comparable prior work exists (e.g., Marx et al.'s own use of TCGA-COAD for partial validation), it is acknowledged and distinguished on method rather than presented as though this project were the first to attempt any part of this.

This review was conducted to ground and contextualize the already-completed computational study, per the explicit instruction that it should not reopen or expand the analytical scope. No methodological changes were made to the frozen results as a result of this review. No genuinely critical factual or methodological error was identified in the course of this literature search; the one terminology refinement it motivates (referring to the Marx et al. genes as an "externally nominated panel" rather than an "EOCRC signature") is a labeling precision already reflected in the frozen project record's own language, not a change to any result.
